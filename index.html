01  The project
Client
Cloudsort, US
Role
Design lead, v1.5 → v3.0
Span
2022 — present
Team
Product lead + engineering

A marketplace that had grown for three years without a spine.

US trucking companies sell unused freight capacity here; shippers buy it to move loads. I joined at version 1.5 and have been the design lead through three major versions since.

02  The problem
Three symptoms, observed on calls with dispatchers and buyers.

The product was organised around the system, not the person using it.

Navigation described what the software did rather than what someone was trying to get done. Three years of features had accumulated without anyone stepping back to name the spine.

People asked a teammate instead of the interface.
New users could not tell which section held the thing they needed, so the fastest path became a message to whoever had been there longer.
The same job lived in three places.
Capacity could be found, compared and bought through different routes that had each been added for a different release.
Version 1.0 looked like its database.
Tables, IDs and status codes on screen; the reasoning a dispatcher does in their head was nowhere in the layout.
v1.0 — the structure I inherited
v1.5 — first pass, before the spine existed
03  The decision
Tested clean, shipped wrong, rebuilt. The call I would defend in any review.

A modal tested well and still broke the job.

Lane detail opened in a modal. In testing it read clean — until a buyer wanted to compare two lanes. The modal covered the map, so comparing meant closing, remembering, reopening. I rebuilt it as a panel docked to the side, and the map never disappears again.

Killed
Modal over the map. One lane at a time, context gone.
Shipped
Docked panel. Map holds, lanes compare side by side.
What I took from it: a pattern can pass a usability test and still fail the job, because tests hand people one task at a time and real work arrives two at once.
04  The work
My Resources. Sellers stopped hunting for their own capacity — everything they own sits in one place, in the order they think about it.
Purchases & Billing. Money questions answered without leaving the product or asking finance.
Scanning, on a phone. Designed for one hand in a warehouse, where the other hand is holding the freight.
05  Also shipped
Seventeen or more surfaces went out over three years. The ones worth naming:
Navbar and menu system
Payment method flow
Calendar reminders
Dispatch scheduling
Unregistered-user onboarding
Transactional email templates
Demand Signals
Superuser v3 admin panel
06  Honestly
Cloudsort has not published usage figures, so there are none here. What I can point to is duration: three years, three major versions, and a weekly build review with engineering that never stopped.

01  The project
Client
Pacific Health Ventures
Role
Sole product designer
Span
2025 — present
Partners
Three organisations

A wellness platform built for the years that matter.

Two applications for senior living communities. Staff run physical and cognitive assessments on iPad; residents get activity tracking, a vitality score and an AI companion. One system, built from a blank page, for two groups who could not be more different.

02  The problem
Research was run by the HumanGood team. I joined the calls and turned findings into flows.

One system serving a clinician and an eighty-year-old.

Staff are trained, fast, and interrupted constantly — they need precision and speed. Residents are not trained, not in a hurry, and are frequently reading without their glasses. The same data has to serve both without either interface feeling like a compromise.

An assessment cannot be paused politely.
Sit-to-stand tests are timed and physical. If the staff member has to look for a control mid-test, the measurement is already worse.
Consent is part of the product, not paperwork around it.
Health data in a residential setting means the flow has to make agreement explicit and reversible, in plain language.
A score can motivate or it can frighten.
Vitality had to read as encouragement on a resident's screen and as data on a clinician's — same number, two registers.
03  The decision
Two apps rather than one app with two modes.

I split the product in two rather than build one app with a switch.

A single app with a staff mode would have been cheaper to build and worse for everyone. Every shared control would have been a compromise between a gloved, hurried tap and a slow, careful one. Two applications on one data model let each interface be sized, paced and worded for the person holding it.

Staff · iPad
Choosing an assessment. Dense, fast, everything reachable without a menu.
Staff · in the room
Sit-to-stand, timed. One primary action, sized for a glance while watching a person move.
04  The work
The master flow. Both apps mapped as one system before a single screen was styled — the only way to see where staff and resident paths touch.
Low fidelity, where the argument gets settled
The same flows carried to final UI
05  Honestly
Most of this product is under NDA and will stay that way. The marketing site and two staff views are cleared; the resident app, the vitality model and the companion are not, so they are described rather than shown. The platform is in active development, so there are no outcome figures to quote yet.

01  The project
Client
Bavox, Switzerland
Role
Sole designer
Span
8 months, 2023
Languages
German, French

I did not know what a Baujournal was.

A construction-management platform for Swiss architects and contractors — projects, tasks, contacts and the paperwork that lives on a building site. I designed all of it, in a trade and a language I had to learn as I went.

02  The problem
The client brought the domain. I brought the questions.

A tool that speaks the wrong language is invisible.

Architects were running projects across email, WhatsApp and paper journals kept on site. Tools existed, but they were generic, built elsewhere, and localised badly enough that nobody trusted them with a real project.

The record of the day was on paper, on site.
Whatever happened at the building got written in a journal and typed up later, or not at all.
Vocabulary was not decoration.
Swiss construction has exact words for exact documents. Getting a label approximately right is the same as getting it wrong.
German is longer than English.
Compounds run to twenty-five characters and more, so any layout designed to English measure breaks on the first real label.
Where the product actually gets used
One hand, bright sun, gloves on
03  The decision
Design in German first, then let the other languages sit inside it.

I designed the longest language first.

The usual order is to design in English and translate afterwards, which is how you end up with truncated buttons and two-line labels in the shipped product. I built every layout against real German terms taken from the client's own documents, so French and English arrived as the easy cases rather than the exceptions.

Designed to English
Baustellenjournal
Button sized for "Site log" truncates it
Designed to German
Baustellen­journal
Holds in all three languages
04  The work
Project detail. Everything about one building in one place, in the order an architect asks for it — not in the order the database stores it.
Tasks. The board a foreman can read from a phone in daylight, with columns that match how work actually moves on a site.
05  Honestly
I have no adoption numbers for Bavox. What I know is that it shipped in eight months with one designer, and that Swiss architects are still using it three years later — which, for a tool a trade did not ask for, is the result I would have wanted.

Screening task, not shipped work
Client
Kraymer Arts
Role
Solo, end to end
Span
2025
Focus

The homepage argued before it seduced.

A real store selling handmade anime fine jewellery — rings, pendants and necklaces cast from the universes people grew up on. I was given the homepage and asked what I would change.

02  The problem
A fandom does not buy craft. It buys belonging.

It proved the jewellery was handmade to someone who did not want a piece yet.

The original sequence led with craftsmanship, materials and process — the arguments you make to a visitor who is already deciding between two rings. The anime buyer has not got that far. They arrive wanting the character, the series, the memory.

Nothing on the first screen named a universe.
A fan looking for their series had no way to see that it was stocked without searching for it.
The story sat where the products should have been.
Brand narrative earns attention after desire exists, not before it.
Proof was doing the seducing.
Reviews and guarantees were high on the page, working hard to reassure a visitor who had not yet found anything to want.
03  The decision
Nine sections, re-ordered around desire.

Identity first, product second, craft third.

I did not redesign the page so much as re-sequence it. Universes come first so a fan sees themselves immediately, best sellers follow so desire has somewhere to land, and the craft story moves down to where it converts a person already holding a decision.

How I work · 01

How I design with AI

The fastest pair of hands I have ever worked with. The taste, the judgment, and the responsibility are still mine.

The short version

A tool with no opinion of its own

AI compresses the distance between an idea and a testable thing — it does not decide what is worth testing. Before AI, an idea had to survive hours of Figma work before anyone could react to it. Now a client clicks a working version the same afternoon the idea shows up. The ideas did not get better on their own; they just stopped waiting in line.

The studio behind that sentence is very concrete: a small stack of tools with sharply divided jobs, one fast loop, and a short list of things that never get delegated.

01 · the stack

Six seats, one desk.

Every tool has a sharply divided job — and none of them get a vote on what is worth building. That seat stays mine.

Claude
the builder
My main partner for turning a described flow into working, clickable code — this whole site included. I direct in plain language, it builds live, and I redline the result round after round. It is where ideas become things you can actually click the same afternoon.
prototypes · code · copy · research digests
Figma MCP
the bridge
Connects the AI directly to my real Figma files — it reads actual frames, components and tokens instead of guessing. So generated work speaks my design system’s language from the first draft, and nothing drifts off-brand.
live file context · design tokens · components
Figma
home base
Still where the real design lives. Everything AI proposes gets pulled back here to be refined by hand, wired into the system, and made production-ready. AI never gets the final say — this canvas does.
source of truth · handoff · polish
Higgsfield
the image lab
For generative visuals, art direction and mood — hero imagery, textures, motion references. When a screen needs a feeling before it needs pixels, this is where I audition the look.
imagery · mood · art direction
ChatGPT
the sparring partner
A second brain for structure and stress-testing — reframing a problem, arguing the opposite side, drafting research plans, cleaning up long documents. When I want a different angle than my main builder gives, I go here.
strategy · reframing · synthesis
The rotating bench
Every new tool gets one week to earn its seat — image models, voice, agents, whatever ships next. Most do not keep it. The stack stays small on purpose.
02 · the loop

Idea to clickable, same afternoon.

01
Frame
I write the problem and the one question the screen must answer — in plain words, by hand. No tool touches this step.
02
Generate
Claude turns it into working code, or Higgsfield into imagery — three rough directions in the time one used to take.
03
React
I click it like a stranger would, or walk the AI through it as a confused first-time user, and mark what breaks.
04
Refine
Back into Figma over the Figma-MCP bridge — wired to real components and tokens, made production-ready by hand.
05
Decide
Taste and evidence pick the winner. The model proposes; I dispose. Then the loop runs again.
The loop is fast so the taste gets more at-bats — rounds three and four repeat until it feels right.

04 · the rules

What never gets delegated.

AI proposes. Evidence and taste dispose.
Nothing ships that only AI has looked at.
“Does this feel right?” is not delegable.
Speed buys more explorations — never less care.
The client hears my voice, not a model’s.

AI has never stood in a warehouse watching a driver scan one-handed, and it was not on the calls where one word made a feature invisible. That is why the judgment stays home.

How I work · 02

How I design UX flows

A flow is not a sequence of screens. It is a conversation — and real conversations get interrupted.

The short version

A flow is a conversation

Every flow I design starts with one sentence: “The user is here to ___.” If a screen does not serve that sentence, it does not matter how nice it looks — it is in the way.

And because real conversations get interrupted, I design for the interrupted run: escape hatches on every screen, state you can always see, no traps. Happy paths are fiction — good flows survive reality. That thinking runs through Cloudsort, Focus and Bavox alike.

But this is easier to show than to tell. The cloud portfolio went through research, brainstorming and flow mapping before a single cloud got drawn — and I kept the boards.

01 · research

Who lands on a designer’s portfolio — and what do they need in the first minute?

How I work · 03

How I design UI

The last mile of all the thinking — where it either becomes invisible or gets in the way.

The short version

Hierarchy, calm, and a hundred quiet decisions

Every screen answers one question first — the rest of the layout is ranked by how much it helps answer it. Density gets rhythm instead of borders. And the finish lives in decisions no screenshot shows: a word, a touch target, a shadow. That is what “beautiful” means to me in UI — not decoration. Fewer questions.

Claims are cheap, so I left the workshop door open on the cloud portfolio: the components, the colors, the type, the motion — all of it live and inspectable. What follows is that system, written down.

01 · foundations

A scale, not a pile of sizes.

Six steps carry every screen. If a new size is needed, the hierarchy is usually wrong somewhere else.

Exchange. A map, a list and a detail panel on one screen, held apart by spacing rather than boxes inside boxes.

02 · color

One blue, every altitude.

There was exactly one hue on that site. Hierarchy came from lightness, not from new colors — ink was night-sky blue, accents were noon blue, and the background was the sky itself.

Ink at six altitudes — one hue, six lightnesses, no second color anywhere.
A hub built for daily use — tight, quiet, repeatable
A marketing grid — more air, because it is read once
03 · motion

Nothing snaps. Everything floats.

Four moves animated the entire site — all on the same easing curve, just at different sizes and speeds.

Drift
52–74s · ease-in-out · alternate
The sky never sits still — four clouds drift behind every page, slowly enough to miss.
Puff in
Entry · one curve, every element

04 · decisions

The rules I argued myself into.

Everything is a pill or a rounded card — clouds do not have corners.
Ink is never black. It is night-sky blue.
One hue total. Hierarchy is lightness, never a second color.
Shadows are tinted blue. Gray shadows would turn the day off.
Glass panels everywhere — the sky should read through every layer.
One face for the pencil, one for the pen, one for the letter.

Written for the cloud portfolio, and the reason this one looks nothing like it: same discipline, a different set of rules for a different room.
